THE BUILD DECISION RECORD

A “no” is a deliverable too

1 Request and requester

5 Verdict: Build, Pilot or Don’t build

2 Decision, person, moment

6 Alternative offered instead

3 Five scores, with one-line reasons

7 Trigger or date to revisit

4 Problem cost vs 5-year cost

8 Signed: clinical owner, interface lead, you

Whatever the verdict, you write it down in a Build Decision Record. One page, eight fields. One: the request, and who asked. Two: the decision it’s meant to change — the person and the moment. Three: the five Build Check scores, each with a one-line reason. Four: the cost of the problem next to the five-year cost of the integration. Five: the verdict — Build, Pilot, or Don’t build yet. Six: if it’s not a full build, the alternative you’re offering instead. A report, a workflow change, a pilot, a lighter pattern. Seven: the trigger or the date to look at it again. “We’ll revisit when the lab moves to the new instrument,” or “in six months.” Eight: signatures — the clinical owner, the interface lead, and you. I want to spend a moment on the “no.” Saying no to an integration request is uncomfortable, especially when it comes from a senior clinician. But a well-documented no is one of the most valuable things you can deliver. It protects your team’s time for the projects that matter, and it’s not a dead end: fields six and seven tell the requester exactly what they get instead, and what would change the answer. You’re not a gatekeeper. You’re looking after limited engineering time — yours included. [Optional: share a time you said no — or wish you had — and what it saved or cost.]
